How we tested it

1. Record36 keys (a to z, 0 to 9), 25 presses each, on a MacBook. Recorded by a phone beside it, and through a Zoom call.
Click Listen to hear the picture of each press.
2. LearnA neural network sees the sound picture of 80% of the presses and learns which key made each one. 10% are kept to choose when to stop.
No test press is ever used here.
3. TestThe remaining presses, never heard before, are scored once. Chance is 2.8%.
Three runs with different random splits.
4. Stress itTest on later presses only, on a different device, and on a different laptop.

What this tells us

Data. Public recordings of one person typing 25 presses of each of 36 keys on a MacBook Pro, captured by an iPhone beside it and through Zoom (Keystroke-Datasets). The laptop test uses public recordings from two MacBook Pros (R-ASCA).
Model. Each press is cut out, turned into a 64-band sound picture, and classified by a compact network that mixes convolution and attention (CoAtNet style), with time-shift and masking augmentation. CPU only, 150 epochs.
Discipline. The test split is scored once per run. The stopping epoch is chosen on a separate validation split. Results are averaged over several random splits and every run is listed.
Limits. One person, one laptop, one room. Our model is smaller than the one in the paper and trained for fewer epochs. The Listen panel replays held-out presses from the recordings; it does not process anyone's audio.
Credit. The approach follows Harrison, Toreini and Mehrnezhad, "A Practical Deep Learning-Based Acoustic Side Channel Attack on Keyboards" (arXiv 2308.01074). Recordings are theirs (Keystroke-Datasets) and those of a later study (R-ASCA). Code and every run: GitHub.
